I am revising my LinkedIn to better position myself for my job search. Develop a headline and About section that lead with the positioning thesis above and read in my voice. I have provided my existing LinkedIn profile as reference.

## The positioning thesis I want the profile to express

I believe that the people and organizations that thrive in the next five years will do so not because of the AI tools they adopt, but whether they've deployed AI in a way that creates 10x humans rather than replaces them. That is why I am seeking a role where I can use AI to help facilitate human potential, by guiding them towards insights, actions, and critical thinking that are personally and professionally transformative. No cheap AI wrappers around ChatGPT; this is about finding the places where AI has the capacity to do the most good.

## What I'm targeting — must-haves

**1. A connection to human psychology.** Not necessarily B2C or B2B2C; it's about the nature of the problem space. I was excited to work at FlagshipRTL because demand planning is fundamentally about predicting human behavior and wants. I'm interested in a company like Styri (https://styri.ai/) because customer retention is about ROI, yes, but it's also about how people *feel* about what you're selling.

**2. Old problem spaces ripe for disruption.** I was also excited to work at Flagship because the problem (demand forecasting) is as old as retail, but no one has solved it well. Styri is working on post-sales, another old-as-capitalism problem. Clairity was trying to predict breast cancer risk, replacing simple mathematical scoring systems that haven't been updated in decades. I'm not as interested in category creation; I prefer to transform an existing category.

**3. Teaching, or a teacher's mindset.** I get the most joy from being a teacher. This
doesn't mean a classroom — fundamentally my goal is to use my expertise to level up
others and make them more successful. That's why I launched Powered Analysis
(https://www.powered-analysis.com/). Two refinements that matter: I want to be involved
at the *strategy* level (what is worth teaching at all, and why), not delivering someone
else's curriculum. And I'm at my best with **electives, not requirements** — curious
people who chose to be there, not audiences I have to convince.

**4. My expertise treated as decision-making authority.** I don't need to manage people. I need my expert voice taken seriously enough that I can act on it. The diagnostic question I now ask every prospective employer: *"If I tell you your idea has a problem, how will you react?"* The answer has to be "thank you." Related test: does this team default to **"yes, and — let's test it and gather data"** or to "no, here's why"?

**5. Hub, not owner.** I'm drawn to cross-functional coordination, customer education,
customer success, and solutions roles where I'm the conduit of information between
engineering, product, and the customer, and my job is to give others the tools to
succeed on their own. I do not want accountability for outcomes I have no authority
over.

**6. An employer that supports visible thought leadership.** Powered Analysis is my laboratory and my thought-leadership platform, not an escape hatch — working with a real team and real resources feeds it. An employer threatened by it is a walking red flag for me.

## What I don't want

you think is too candid to signal publicly.)*

- **Accountability for outcomes without influence over decisions.** This is the single clearest pattern in my career history. Director of Analytics is the archetype and it's the reason I stopped doing it.
- **Generic Chief of Staff roles.** Attractive in theory, but at most company sizes it collapses into "the operator who does the things no one else is responsible for."
- **The RevOps treadmill / quota-carrying roles.** I like the puzzle and the fast feedback loops, but it's also exactly where my anxiety spikes: existentially important when revenue is good, terrible when it stalls, and the oscillation is constant.
- **Healthcare and insurance.** I've landed there twice and I don't care about the outcomes, which makes it hard to care about the work.
- **Purely deterministic technical problem spaces** — supply chain optimization, data normalization. Technically interesting to a data scientist, disconnected from human agency, and therefore not interesting to me.
- **Pure L&D or tool enablement.** "Here's how to use this database" is not what I want. I want to own what gets taught and why.
- **Agency-mode / kitchen-sink service delivery.** Bespoke one-off projects that don't compound.
- **Organizations that celebrate activity over outcomes.** No written specs, no acceptance criteria, "let's just try it" as an operating philosophy. I need enough rigor in the room that rigor is an asset rather than an obstruction.
